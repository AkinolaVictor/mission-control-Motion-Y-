- DENE is a differential evolution (DE) algorithm for neural ensemble architecture search (NEAS) on image classification tasks, using a two-stage framework.
- Stage 1 generates a group of high-quality CNNs; it uses a clustering-based initialization strategy and a surrogate-based performance ranking strategy to improve diversity and efficiency.
- Stage 2 builds a CNN ensemble with shared shallow layers and multiple heads; it uses a bi-objective DE to balance ensemble accuracy and diversity among heads.
- A surrogate model-based ranking strategy performs pairwise comparison of candidate solutions combined with merge sort, aiming to rank candidate performance without training.
- A diversity measurement for neural ensembles considers both neural architecture diversity and classification performance diversity.
- The neural ensemble uses a multi-head structure with shared shallow layers and different deep layers/units, intended to reduce parameters and computational cost.
- The search space is CNN-based; CNNs are used because of parameter sharing and sparse connections for grid-like data.
- NEAS is described as complex due to discrete search space and black-box features, and expensive because candidate models must be trained before evaluation.
- Experiments compare DENE against state-of-the-art evolutionary NAS and NEAS algorithms on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100.
- Reported results: DENE efficiently generates highly competitive neural ensembles and outperforms existing methods in performance and search time.

- NEAS algorithms are categorized by search space into layer-based, unit-based, and supernet-based approaches.
- By construction method, NEAS approaches are incremental construction (build a small model and expand it) or direct construction (construct a complex ensemble directly).
- Layer-based example: AdaNet fixes nodes per layer and controls layer count; AdaNAS and SAEP, based on AdaNet, cut some inter-layer connections.
- Unit-based search space constructs ensembles by stacking architecture units, each containing several layers and connections.
- Supernet-based search space includes feasible layers, connections, and weights; candidate solutions share training results, saving computation/time.
- Supernet examples: NES, NESS, and MH-NES use a DARTS supernet with linearly stacked L units, each with seven convolutional layers; NESS training follows FairNAS and single-path one-shot NAS; NEAS uses MBConv from EfficientNet; MoViNets uses a MobileNetV3-based supernet and follows TuNAS.
- Incremental construction examples: AdaNet initializes a linear architecture and inserts layers; AdaNAS uses knowledge-distillation training from previously found architectures; SEAP prunes low-value connections under constraints such as accuracy or information entropy.
- Direct construction examples: NES uses random search or regularized evolution with greedy selection to minimize loss; DeepEns and HyperDeepEns use random search; Shu et al. use Monte Carlo sampling and Stein variational gradient descent with regularized diversity; NEAS uses an EA to search K neural networks on one supernet; MoViNets first searches an architecture balancing efficiency and accuracy, then builds an ensemble from two same-architecture networks; another framework generates heterogeneous networks based on MENNDL and selects higher-accuracy ones.
- Diversity mechanisms: MH-NES uses Jensen-Shannon divergence; NEAS considers diversity and accuracy inspired by fixed-size determinantal point processes; Herron et al. measure network differences by output differences on the same dataset and sum pairwise differences.
- Layer sharing reduces ensemble parameters; a split point can control shared layers; MH-NES uses a three-block backbone and shares two blocks.
- ENAS generally evolves a population: initialize random architectures, evaluate fitness, select parents, create offspring via mutation/crossover, replace low-performing architectures, and iterate until a termination criterion.
- ENAS encoding can be fixed-length or variable-length; fixed-length works efficiently with traditional operators, while variable-length can reduce reliance on expert experience but requires custom operators and often a maximum length to limit resources.
- Non-linear architectures can be encoded as adjacency matrices (MA-evolution) or as hybrid fixed-backbone block structures (AmoebaNet).
- Search strategies include EAs and swarm intelligence; GA is common; crossover and mutation determine search behavior; single-point crossover is widely accepted; some modified single-point crossover operators split genotypes into two parts.
- DE, introduced by Storn and Price in 1997, generates candidates by adding a weighted difference between two random population members to a third, followed by uniform crossover and one-to-one selection; it is noted for simplicity, robustness, diversity maintenance, and enhancement via adaptive parameters or hybridization.

- Proposed DENE architecture:
  - DENE is a differential evolution algorithm with a two-stage framework.
  - It explicitly optimizes for both diversity and performance while reducing computational overhead.
  - Clustering-based population initialization generates the initial population, narrows subsequent search scope, and improves efficiency.
  - Initialized solutions are used to train a surrogate model-based performance ranking strategy that evaluates candidate solutions in stage one.
  - Stage one uses DE to generate heterogeneous high-performance single CNNs.
  - Stage two constructs a neural ensemble by optimizing a bi-objective problem trading off diversity and accuracy of the CNN ensemble.
  - Final output is a CNN ensemble with shared blocks and multiple heads.

- DENE is a two-stage differential evolution algorithm for neural ensemble architecture search (NEAS) of CNNs.
- Candidate CNN backbone is based on wide residual network (WRN): four convolution blocks plus one pooling layer; all convolution filters are 3×3.
- Conv1 has one convolution layer expanding channels to 16.
- Conv2–conv4 use repeated convolution units of type (a) or (b); type (b) adds dropout between two convolution layers.
- Channel expansion rules: conv2 expands to 16×k1; conv3 first unit down-samples to 32, later units use 32×k2; conv4 first unit sets 64, later units use 64×k3.
- Fixed-length integer encoding of length 9; each 3-bit group encodes type, expansion factor, and repetition number for conv2, conv3, and conv4.
- Population initialization: uniformly sample candidate solutions, train/test them, encode CNNs and error rates as real vectors, cluster with k-means into k=N clusters, then select solutions closest to cluster centroids to form the initial population.
- Surrogate-based performance ranking uses LightGBM as a pairwise surrogate: input is two concatenated CNN encodings; output indicates which solution is better (0 = former, 1 = latter).
- Surrogate is trained from scratch on initialization candidates and updated every certain generations with newly generated high-quality candidates.
- Ranking combines the surrogate with merge sort (time complexity O(n log2 n), space complexity O(n)).
- The surrogate estimates relative pairwise quality rather than directly estimating accuracy.
- DE mutation has two types: a global architecture mutation using random neighbors, scaling factor F, probability r, and a direction vector based on previous best solutions; and a local mutation changing one bit.
- DE crossover has two types, each chosen with 50% probability: block-level crossover exchanging one 3-bit convolution block with probability CR, and standard bit-level crossover with probability CR.

- Stage two searches CNN ensemble architectures using a layer-sharing strategy: three convolution blocks are fixed as shared layers; for an ensemble with m heads, m different convolution blocks and pooling layers are selected from the fourth convolution blocks and pooling layers of candidate CNNs.
- The ensemble construction task is modeled as a bi-objective optimization problem: maximize ensemble accuracy and maximize diversity.

- Diversity objective: combines Euclidean distance between head architectures and the number of samples with different classification results across heads, as defined in Eq. 8.
- Encoding: an integer array of length m, where each element is an index of a candidate CNN from stage one; the first element identifies the CNN contributing shared layers, and elements 2 through m identify CNNs constructing head architectures.
- Optimization uses Pareto dominance for the two objectives (accuracy and diversity), with population updates based on neighbor-based differential evolution and binomial crossover.

- DENE is a two-stage differential evolution (DE) algorithm for neural ensemble architecture search.
- Candidate solutions are architectures; DE operators generate offspring; environmental selection evaluates candidates on accuracy and diversity.
- Selection uses crowdedness-based non-dominated sorting with Pareto dominance as primary criterion and crowding distance as secondary tiebreaker within the same front.
- Elitist preservation combines parent and offspring populations; aims to retain true Pareto-optimal solutions, prevent genetic drift/premature convergence, and maintain selection pressure without problem-specific parameter tuning.
- The Pareto front represents trade-offs between accuracy and diversity; non-dominated solutions are preferentially retained.
- Final selection chooses the solution with highest classification accuracy from the external archive, decodes it to a CNN ensemble, and outputs it.
- Initialization: random sampling of candidate solutions followed by k-means clustering; complexity O(q N^2), where q = k-means iterations and N = population size.
- DE operator generation/mating selection complexity: O(N).
- First stage uses a GBDT surrogate model to compare two solutions (binary superior/inferior); training complexity O(2 k N log N), where k = number of trees.
- First-stage performance evaluation combines merge sorting with surrogate model; complexity O(N log N); overall first stage O(2 T k N log N), where T = iterations.
- Second stage uses crowdedness-based non-dominated sorting; complexity O(N^2); stage complexity O(T N^2).
- Overall time complexity: O(T N^2), consistent with many population-based optimization algorithms; surrogate model reduces neural network training time/execution time.
- Benchmarks: CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100.
- Experimental settings: PyTorch 1.6.0; Intel Core i7-9700K CPU, NVIDIA RTX2080 GPU, 32GB memory.
- DE crossover rate is set above 0.5 for stronger global search due to complex architecture search.
- Number of heads m tested: 3, 5, 10. Parameter count increases with m; error rate negatively correlates with m. m=3 has less ideal error; m=5 has more than twice parameters of m=3 but close error rates; m=10 has lower error than m=5 but huge parameter count. m=5 used unless otherwise specified.
- Layer sharing ablation: without layer sharing, parameter count is 6.96x larger on CIFAR-10 and 4.84x larger on CIFAR-100; layer sharing yields significantly better error rates on both datasets.
- Population initialization ablation: clustering-based initialization yields better average and best error rates than random initialization.
- Performance ranking ablation: surrogate-based ranking saves 13.70% model search time on CIFAR-10 and 18.10% on CIFAR-100; no significant error-rate difference versus fully training all candidate CNNs.
- Results comparison: DENE produces a small model on CIFAR-10 and a relatively large-scale model on CIFAR-100; larger than DeepEns, NES-RS, HyperDeepEns, and MH-NES; achieves lower classification error on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 than other ensemble models; among compared ENAS algorithms, best average and lowest error on CIFAR-10 and lowest average error on CIFAR-100 (text truncated).

- DENE is a two-stage differential evolution (DE) algorithm for neural ensemble architecture search (NEAS), designed to automatically build multi-head neural ensembles for image classification.
- Stage 1 generates high-accuracy CNNs using a single-objective search for rapid convergence.
- Stage 2 constructs the ensemble using a multi-objective search balancing accuracy and diversity; it is a small-scale combinatorial problem with no additional model-training cost because weight parameters are reused.
- The architecture uses a multi-head design with shared layers, which saves computational resources and reduces parameter count.
- A fixed number of shared blocks is a trade-off: increasing layer-sharing patterns would exponentially expand search space and time, limiting ensemble diversity and algorithmic flexibility.
- DE is argued to suit NEAS because its mutation strategy promotes architectural diversity, its parameter control is simple/robust (F and CR), and it converges efficiently; the difference-vector approach encourages exploration of diverse architectures.
- A surrogate-model-based performance ranking strategy uses pairwise comparison of candidate solutions, avoiding the need to accurately predict individual architecture accuracy.
- The surrogate is LightGBM trained on initialization-stage training results; merge sorting ranks candidates. This mitigates accuracy decline from insufficient training samples and reduces computational requirements.
- The surrogate strategy significantly reduces execution time without compromising accuracy compared with full training of each candidate; stability can vary with input data characteristics and configuration, and LightGBM categorical handling/subsampling may introduce variability.
- Experiments on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 report DENE with lower classification error than DeepEns, NES-RS, HyperDeepEns, and MH-NES; compared with ENAS algorithms, DENE has the best average and lowest error on CIFAR-10 and the lowest average error on CIFAR-100.
- p-values indicate DENE significantly outperforms compared NEAS algorithms on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 and has significantly better error rate than three ENAS algorithms; NSGANet and NESBS are close, while EEEA-Net has the lowest best error on CIFAR-100 but lacks independent replicated statistics.
- Search time: 17.0 GPU hours on CIFAR-10 and 18.1 GPU hours on CIFAR-100; lower than MH-NES but higher than DeepEns and NES-RS; generated model parameter count is larger than DeepEns and NES-RS; initialization training time is included.
- DENE is described as achieving a good balance between classification accuracy and execution time; NSGANet, DeepMaker, and SaMuNet are omitted from one comparison because their execution times are much longer.
- Case study on a CIFAR-100 leopard image: NESBS misclassifies as tiger with confidence 0.228 and focuses on part of the outline; DENE correctly classifies as leopard with confidence 0.803 and focuses on the body/spots, suppressing background interference.
- Generalization test uses CIFAR-100 split into 20 hyper-classes, with 3 classes per hyper-class for training and 2 for testing, creating data shift; DENE shows thinner, lower error-rate boxes than DeepMaker, NSGANet, DeepEns, NES-RS, and MH-NES, indicating superior generalization.
- Increasing base classifiers from m=5 to m=10 improves generalization; DENE has the lowest and most stable error rates on shifted data.
- Real-world tests: FruitNet (six labels, varying fruit quality) and Architectural Heritage Elements Dataset (imbalanced classes, largest category about 4.7 times smallest); DENE achieves lowest error on both and lowest average error on AHE, indicating good generalization and robustness.
- Uncertainties include internal (surrogate accuracy, exploration/exploitation balance), external (dataset shift, evaluation noise), parametric (weights/hyperparameters), and non-parametric (architecture/activation choices); mitigated by the two-stage approach, surrogate model, and diversity-aware optimization, but stochasticity and search-space complexity remain.
- Future directions include efficient algorithms with variable shared layers and training-free methods to reduce computational burden and enhance efficiency.

- A two-stage differential evolution algorithm for neural ensemble architecture search reportedly has competitive performance versus state-of-the-art evolutionary NAS and NEAS algorithms.
- It uses a multi-head architecture with shared layers, which saves computational resources and reduces the number of parameters.
- A surrogate model-based performance ranking strategy reduces consumption of computing resources and searching time.
- A clustering-based initialization strategy improves classification accuracy of the CNN ensemble.
- Future work includes improving diversity of head architectures, designing a more flexible layer-sharing strategy, and adapting the surrogate model to different search spaces and architecture search tasks to improve generalization.
