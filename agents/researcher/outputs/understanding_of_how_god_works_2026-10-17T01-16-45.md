# Understanding of How God works

Understanding the "works of God" is a multidisciplinary pursuit that spans systematic theology, philosophical inquiry, and empirical psychological research. Because divine action is often described as exceeding human reasoning—as noted in Isaiah 55:8-9—different frameworks have emerged to categorize, detect, and interpret how a higher power interacts with the created world.

## Theological Frameworks of Divine Action

In traditional biblical studies, the works of God are generally categorized by their function and purpose. According to [Bible Hub](https://biblehub.com/topical/u/understanding_the_works_of_god.htm), these can be divided into several core domains:

*   **Creation and Providence:** The initial act of omnipotence (Creation) and the ongoing maintenance of the universe (Providence).
*   **Redemption and Sanctification:** The process of reconciliation via Christ and the subsequent moral refinement of the individual.
*   **Judgment and Miracles:** The application of moral accountability and the exercise of divine authority through supernatural interventions to foster belief.

### The Concept of "Walking with God"
The biblical narrative provides specific examples of how individuals experience the workings of God. The life of Enoch is often cited as a model of a close, communicative relationship, termed "walking with God." Pastor Mike Henry of Pleasant View Baptist Church suggests that such relationships are often triggered by significant life catalysts; for Enoch, this coincided with the birth of his son, Methuselah. This relationship was so profound that Enoch is one of only two figures in the Bible noted for not experiencing physical death, as "God took him."

## Epistemological Approaches to Understanding the Divine

How humans "know" or "understand" God's work varies based on the epistemological lens applied—whether through faith, reason, or a synthesis of both.

### Grounded Theology (GT)
A modern approach to this understanding is the **Grounded Theology (GT)** framework developed by G. J. Amundson. Unlike standard Grounded Theory, which relies on an objective observer, GT views the researcher as an active participant. It integrates:
1.  **General Revelation:** Empirical sociological data.
2.  **Special Revelation:** Scriptural authority.

Knowledge in GT is formed via "co-constructivism"—a dialogue between empirical data, the researcher, and the Holy Spirit—utilizing deductive, inductive, and abductive reasoning to reach spiritual discernment.

### Philosophical Perspectives
Philosophers have proposed diverse models for the nature of the divine, which fundamentally change how "God's work" is interpreted:
*   **Substance Monism:** Baruch Spinoza proposed *Deus sive Natura* (God or Nature), suggesting that God is not a separate creator but the one infinite substance of the universe. In this view, everything that happens is a modification of God's attributes.
*   **Transcendental Idealism:** Immanuel Kant argued that God is a "noumenon"—a thing-in-itself that cannot be known through theoretical reason but is accessible via moral consciousness.
*   **Coaxialism:** Sorin Cerin posits that absolute truth is unattainable for humans due to the "Illusion of Life," suggesting that the Creator is "Aleatory" and exists as the opposite of infinity.

## Empirical and Psychological Observations

While theology focuses on the *nature* of God, psychology and health sciences focus on the *experience* of the divine.

### Spiritual Experiences in Crisis
A scoping review of 133 studies published in the *Indian Journal of Palliative Care* explored how cancer patients perceive the works of God. The findings indicate a complex dichotomy:
*   **Positive Integration:** Many patients view God as a healer or support system, often seeing their physicians as "divine instruments." This frequently leads to "posttraumatic growth" and renewed resilience.
*   **Spiritual Struggle:** Conversely, some experience "spiritual struggles," characterized by feelings of doubt or perceived abandonment by the divine during their illness.

### Neurological and Social Theories
Secular research suggests that the understanding of God may be a product of human biology or social evolution. Some theories propose that "watchful gods" evolved to enforce group morality, while neurological research from Johns Hopkins links certain spiritual experiences to chemical triggers (such as DMT) or temporal lobe seizures.

## Critiques and Divergent Views

Not all frameworks conclude that God "works" in a traditional, benevolent, or organized manner.

*   **Pyrotheology:** Peter Rollins advocates for a "religionless Christianity" that celebrates ambiguity. He argues that beliefs promising absolute certainty about how God works are actually "idols" that hinder true spiritual growth.
*   **The Monomyth:** Joseph Campbell viewed "God" as a metaphor for the mystery of existence. He argued that theists err by mistaking these metaphors for facts, while atheists err by mistaking them for lies.
*   **Antitheism:** Figures like Christopher Hitchens argued that organized religious interpretations of God's work are often irrational and used to justify violence.

## Conclusion

Understanding how God works requires navigating a spectrum between the empirical and the eternal. For the theologian, it is a matter of scriptural revelation and "walking" in faith. For the philosopher, it is a question of ontology and the limits of human reason. For the psychologist, it is an observation of how the *belief* in divine action facilitates human resilience or causes spiritual distress. Ultimately, whether viewed as the "first uncaused cause," the "infinite substance," or a personal guide, the understanding of divine work remains a central point of tension between human observation and faith.
